# Guide 5: Expense Management, Cost Analysis, and Profitability

> All figures are **fictional**; thresholds illustrative. Not accounting advice.

## 1. Cost types

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Does not change with volume in the short run | Rent, salaries (often), insurance |
| Variable | Changes with volume | Materials, packaging, sales commissions, delivery per order |
| Semi-variable | Fixed base plus variable part | Electricity, phone plans |
| Direct | Traceable to a product/service | Raw materials, direct labour |
| Indirect (overhead) | Shared across products | Rent, admin salaries |

Classification depends on time horizon and business; a salary is "fixed" in a month but can be adjusted over a year.

## 2. Operating expenses vs capital expenditure

Operating expenses (opex) are consumed in running the business (rent, wages). **Capital expenditure (capex)** buys assets lasting multiple periods (machinery, vehicles). Capex is usually spread over the asset's life (depreciation) rather than expensed at once; treatment depends on accounting rules and tax law `[SOURCE NEEDED]`. Recording capex as opex makes one month look unprofitable and later months look better.

## 3. Cost categorisation

Use a consistent chart of accounts: COGS, operating (payroll, rent, utilities, marketing, admin), financing (interest), tax, capex, owner drawings. Few, clear categories beat many overlapping ones. "Miscellaneous" above ~5–10% of expenses (CONFIG) hides information.

## 4. Budget versus actual

`Variance = Actual − Budget`. For expenses, positive = overspend. Distinguish **timing** variance (annual fee paid early) from **real** overrun. Review monthly; reforecast when assumptions change.

**Fictional:** marketing budget 10,000; actual 12,500 ⇒ +2,500 (25% over). Check whether sales rose accordingly before judging.

## 5. Expense growth

Growth in expenses is **not automatically bad**. Compare against revenue growth, volume (cost per unit/order), and plans (new store, new hire).

| Revenue growth | Expense growth | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| +15% | +5% | Operating leverage (check not under-investing) |
| +5% | +15% | Margin pressure: investigate |
| −5% | +5% | Concern: costs not following activity |
| +20% | +20% | Proportional: margin stable |

Expense-to-revenue ratio = expenses ÷ revenue (only meaningful if both cover the same period and basis).

## 6. Profit margins

Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue; operating margin = operating profit ÷ revenue; net margin = net profit ÷ revenue (see Guide 1, rules FIN-007 to FIN-009). Compare against own history and similar businesses; there is no universal "good" margin.

## 7. Contribution margin and break-even

- Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit.
- **Break-even units** = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.

**Fictional:** fixed costs 120,000/year; price 100; variable cost 60 ⇒ contribution 40 ⇒ break-even = 120,000 ÷ 40 = **3,000 units** ⇒ break-even revenue = 300,000. Selling 4,000 units ⇒ profit = 4,000 × 40 − 120,000 = **40,000**.

**Margin of safety** = (actual − break-even) ÷ actual = (4,000 − 3,000) ÷ 4,000 = 25%.

**Limitations:** assumes constant price and variable cost, one product (or a fixed mix), and fixed costs that stay fixed across the range.

## 8. Unit economics

Profit per unit/order/customer after all variable costs. **Fictional order:** average order 500; product cost 300; delivery 40; payment fees 10; packaging 15 ⇒ contribution 135 per order (27%). If the customer-acquisition cost is 150, the first order loses 15; profitability depends on repeat purchases (needs retention data, Guide 4).

## 9. Cost allocation

Shared costs (rent, admin) can be allocated by revenue, space, headcount, or activity. Allocation choices change reported product profit; use them for insight, not as precise truth. Do not drop a product just because allocated overhead makes it look unprofitable: the overhead may remain.

## 10. Expense anomalies

Unusual amounts, duplicates, new payees, odd timing, category shifts. An anomaly is a **prompt to check**, not a conclusion. Legitimate causes: annual payments, one-off repairs, new supplier, expansion, seasonal buying, corrected entries. See rules ANO.

## 11. Cost-reduction strategies and their risks

| Strategy | Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Renegotiate supplier prices / consolidate | Lower unit cost | Supplier concentration, quality |
| Reduce waste, returns, rework | Direct savings | Needs measurement |
| Energy/utility efficiency | Ongoing savings | Upfront cost |
| Cut marketing | Immediate saving | May reduce future sales |
| Reduce headcount/hours | Large saving | Service quality, morale, legal obligations `[SOURCE NEEDED]` |
| Defer maintenance | Saves now | Breakdown, safety risk, higher future cost |

**Do not cut costs that protect quality, safety, employees, or legal/regulatory compliance** without careful analysis; savings may be smaller than the damage.

## 12. Worked example: margin decline

Q1: revenue 100,000, COGS 55,000 (gross margin 45%); Q2: revenue 110,000, COGS 66,000 (40%). Gross profit rose 45,000 → 44,000 despite higher revenue. Possible causes: supplier price rise, discounts, mix shift, wrong cost entries, inventory costing change. Next checks: unit cost trend by product, average selling price, mix, discount totals.
