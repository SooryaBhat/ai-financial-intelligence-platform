# Guide 7: Business Planning and Decision-Making

> All figures are **fictional**. Frameworks organise thinking; they are **not a substitute for evidence**.

## 1. Defining the problem

State the problem as an observable, measurable gap: "Gross margin fell from 45% to 40% between Q1 and Q2" rather than "costs are out of control". Define scope (which product, period, location), the evidence available, and who is affected. Avoid embedding a cause in the problem statement.

## 2. Measurable objectives

Use SMART-style objectives: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Example: "Raise gross margin from 40% to 43% by end of Q4 without reducing monthly unit sales by more than 5%." State the baseline, the metric definition (rule ID), the data source, and the review date.

## 3. Root-cause analysis

**Five Whys:** ask "why" repeatedly until a controllable cause appears.
Example: Margin fell → why? Unit costs rose → why? Supplier raised prices → why? We bought in small lots → why? Cash was tight.
**Limitations:** follows one chain and can reflect the analyst's assumptions; different people reach different answers; "why" five times may stop at opinion. Verify each step with data.

**Fishbone (Ishikawa):** group possible causes (People, Process, Materials, Pricing, Equipment, Environment, Measurement) around the problem. **Limitations:** produces hypotheses, not findings; needs data to test which are real; can become a long list without priorities.

Always finish with: "What data would confirm or reject this cause?"

## 4. SWOT analysis

Strengths and weaknesses (internal), opportunities and threats (external). Useful for discussion and strategy framing. **Limitations:** qualitative, subjective, often generic. Make each item specific and evidence-linked ("top 3 customers = 55% of revenue", not "customer risk"). Convert key items to objectives and risks.

## 5. Scenario analysis

Define 3 scenarios (base, downside, upside) with explicit assumptions and compute outcomes on key metrics (profit, cash). Choose the decision that performs acceptably across scenarios, and identify **trigger indicators** to know which scenario is unfolding. See Guide 2 §13 for cash scenarios.

## 6. Risk assessment

Score likelihood (1–5) × impact (1–5). Example:
| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Score | Response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Largest customer delays payment | 3 | 4 | 12 | Credit terms, deposits |
| Key supplier stops delivery | 2 | 5 | 10 | Second supplier |
| Power outage halts production | 4 | 2 | 8 | Backup, insurance |
Scores are judgments, not measurements; document the reasoning and revisit regularly. Responses: avoid, reduce, transfer (insurance), accept.

## 7. Prioritising business problems

Rank by (a) size of the impact (amount at stake), (b) urgency, (c) confidence in the diagnosis, (d) effort/cost to fix, (e) dependencies. Fix data problems first when they make other conclusions unreliable.

## 8. Cost-benefit analysis

Compare the costs and benefits of an option over time, in money where possible, including implementation cost, ongoing cost, risk, and timing.

**Fictional example:** investment 30,000; net benefit 12,000 per year for 3 years.
- Undiscounted: 36,000 − 30,000 = +6,000; payback = 30,000 ÷ 12,000 = 2.5 years.
- Discounted at 10% (illustrative rate): 12,000 ÷ 1.10 = 10,909; ÷ 1.21 = 9,917; ÷ 1.331 = 9,016; total ≈ 29,842 ⇒ NPV ≈ **−158**.
The project looks positive without discounting but roughly breaks even once the time value of money is counted. Benefits are **estimates**; test sensitivity (what if benefit is 20% lower?).

## 9. Decision matrices

Score options against weighted criteria.
| Criterion (weight) | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost (0.4) | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| Risk (0.3) | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Speed (0.3) | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| **Weighted score** | 1.6 + 0.6 + 1.5 = **3.7** | 1.2 + 1.2 + 0.9 = **3.3** | 2.0 + 0.9 + 0.6 = **3.5** |
Weights and scores are subjective; the matrix structures the discussion. Test sensitivity: if risk weight rises, does the ranking change? Do not treat a small score difference as decisive.

## 10. Short-term versus long-term trade-offs

Cutting marketing improves this quarter's profit but may reduce next year's sales; deferring maintenance saves cash now but risks breakdown. State the time horizon and the cost of waiting. Consider cash constraints and survival first, then growth.

## 11. Evidence-based decision-making

1. Define the decision and the metric. 2. Gather relevant data and check its quality. 3. Separate facts, calculations, predictions, and opinions. 4. Consider alternatives and the do-nothing option. 5. Estimate costs, benefits, risks with ranges. 6. Decide, document assumptions, set a review date. 7. Measure results.

## 12. Monitoring whether an intervention worked

Define before acting: metric, baseline (pre-period of equal length), target, review dates, and comparison/control where possible. Beware regression to the mean, seasonality, concurrent changes, and small samples. If sales rose after a promotion, compare with the same period last year and a non-promoted product group before crediting the promotion.

## 13. When to consult a professional

Tax and statutory compliance, accounting policy, audits, legal contracts, employment law, financing and investor documents, valuation, insolvency risk, suspected fraud. Frameworks here do not replace qualified advice.
